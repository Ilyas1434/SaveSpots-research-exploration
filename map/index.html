Data sources & freshness
    What is SaveSpots?
    SaveSpots.org is a harm-reduction initiative that places boxes of naloxone + fentanyl test strips inside everyday Chicago businesses — barbershops, liquor stores, gas stations, libraries — concentrated in neighborhoods with the highest opioid-overdose burden.

    This map is the data backbone that tells us where the need is greatest and where existing naloxone coverage is thinnest, so every box placement decision is evidence-driven.
    The core problem this solves
    57.7% of Chicago opioid deaths occur in the victim's home ZIP code — people are using alone, at home, where a fixed public resource can't reach them. The solution is take-home naloxone: place boxes in stores people already visit in their own neighborhood, so the drug travels home with them before the overdose happens.

    Traditional outreach concentrates near encampments and service sites. That misses the much larger population using privately. This map identifies the stores with the highest reach into high-burden areas with no existing naloxone nearby.
    How the data was built
    Step 1 — Live overdose data. We pull directly from the Cook County Medical Examiner Case Archive (Socrata API, updated daily). This gives us point-level geocoded overdose deaths from 2014 to present — ~10,400 geocoded Chicago cases. Unlike CDPH or CDC dashboards (which lag 1–2 years), this source updates every day.

    Step 2 — Community-area aggregation. Each death is tagged to one of Chicago's 77 community areas via the chi_commarea field. We compute death totals by year and area, plus a "recent burden" score using 2023–2025 deaths.

    Step 3 — Naloxone supply mapping. We geocoded the Cook County Health naloxone-box registry (pharmacies, vending machines, community orgs). Note: the existing supply is heavily suburban — Cicero, Maywood, Bellwood. Chicago itself is severely underserved.

    Step 4 — Coverage gap scoring. For each community area: gap = recent deaths ÷ (nearby naloxone boxes + 1). High gap = high deaths, few boxes.

    Step 5 — Candidate storefronts. We pull active Chicago business licenses (liquor stores, gas stations, barbershops, laundromats, pharmacies — businesses with regular neighborhood foot traffic). Each is scored: score = recent deaths within 400m × coverage gap × blight/hardship × business-type reach weight × transit bonus. Deduped to one candidate per city block.

    Step 6 — Built environment. We pull live 311 reports of vacant/abandoned buildings and graffiti to build a blight surface. Research shows abandoned-building density predicts overdose risk (Oxford AJE 2024) — this layer reinforces placement decisions without requiring Google Street View.

    Step 7 — CDC context. CDC VSRR provisional data adds a lag-corrected Cook County trend. CDC census-tract Drug OD rates add a finer-grained population-normalized surface beneath the raw death points.
    What each map layer shows
    Death heatmap — kernel density of overdose death locations. Brighter red = more deaths per square block. Filterable by year with the slider.

    Death points — individual geocoded deaths. Click for date, age, cause of death. Useful for block-level targeting.

    OD rate (census tract) — CDC Drug OD deaths per 100k population, normalized for tract size. Corrects for the fact that dense neighborhoods have more deaths simply because more people live there.

    Blight hotspots — density of 311-reported vacant/abandoned buildings and graffiti. Correlates with overdose risk and identifies areas where street-level naloxone distribution is also warranted.

    Existing naloxone — known naloxone box locations (Cook County Health registry). Circles = community sites; larger circles = vending machines.

    Placement candidates — top-scored businesses for a new box. Size = priority score. Click any candidate for the full scoring breakdown.
    Caveats
    Reporting lag: the most recent 3–6 months undercount because toxicology reports take time. Year totals for 2025–2026 are incomplete. The trend in the spark chart greys these out.

    Geography: all counts are where the death occurred, not where the person lived. This is the right denominator for box placement — but different from the residence-based numbers CDPH publishes.

    Naloxone registry: the Cook County registry is self-reported and may be incomplete. Some sites may have moved or closed. Field verification is needed before outreach.
